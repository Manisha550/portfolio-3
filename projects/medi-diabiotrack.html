01 — Healthcare · Mobile app

Medi-DiaBioTrack: a tracking app for people managing diabetes, not just a chart of numbers.

A mobile app for logging blood sugar, getting AI-assisted guidance between doctor visits, and booking specialists — designed so the most important number on the screen is never in doubt.

Overview

What the app does

Medi-DiaBioTrack is structured around five jobs: log a blood sugar reading, see the trend behind that reading, ask an AI assistant a practical question about it, follow a food plan built around it, and — when something looks off — book the right specialist without starting a new search from scratch.

Those five jobs map directly to the app's bottom navigation: Home, History, Log (+), AI Assistant, Profile — a five-icon bar that stays constant across every screen so the person never has to relearn where things live while they're mid-task.

Problem

Most tracking apps show data. Few help you act on it.

Diabetes management is a daily, low-drama routine punctuated by moments that actually matter: a reading that's out of range, a meal choice before it's made, a question that comes up at 11pm when no clinic is open. Generic tracker apps are good at the routine logging and bad at the moments — they show a number and stop.

The design problem, then, wasn't "build a glucose log." It was: what does this app do in the sixty seconds after the number looks wrong? That question is what shaped the AI assistant, the food chart, and the doctor-booking flow as much as the tracker itself.

Users

Who this is for

  • Newly diagnosed adults who are still building the habit of checking and logging regularly, and need reassurance as much as data.
  • Long-term patients who already know their patterns and want faster logging plus a clear trend view, not more education.
  • Family members / caregivers who may check in on someone else's readings — a secondary audience this version doesn't fully design for yet (see Next improvements).
Research

Starting from the clinical routine, not the screen

Before wireframing, I mapped the actual clinical routine a diabetes patient follows: test → log → notice a pattern → adjust diet or exercise → escalate to a doctor if the pattern doesn't resolve. Each app section corresponds to one link in that chain, which is why "Suggest Exercise," "How to reduce sugar?," and "Search Best Doctor" sit as quick-reply chips directly inside the AI assistant screen — they're the routine's actual next steps, not generic chatbot suggestions.

Competitive analysis

Where existing trackers fall short

Pattern seen

Most glucose-tracking apps treat the chart as the destination — you log a number, see a line graph, and the interaction ends there.

Design response

I treated the chart as a starting point. Under the graph, "Highest Sugar" and "Lowest Sugar" sit as one-tap summaries, and the AI assistant is one nav-tap away from any reading — the trend always leads somewhere.

Pattern seen

Doctor-booking, when it exists at all, usually lives in a completely separate app.

Design response

Booking is inside the same app, filtered by specialty (Endocrinologist, Diabetologist, Nutritionist), so a concerning trend can end in an appointment in the same session it was noticed.

Persona
S

Sumaiya, 29 — recently diagnosed, Type 2

Works full-time, checks her sugar twice a day, and has started paying attention to what she eats but doesn't yet know which foods are actually the problem.

Goal: understand her own patterns without booking a clinic visit for every question.
Frustration: her current app shows a number with no context — "125 mg/dL" means nothing without knowing if that's trending up or down.
What she needs from this app: a reading that comes with a plain-language read on whether it's normal, and a fast path to a real answer when it isn't.

Information architecture

Five destinations, one persistent bar

The IA is deliberately flat: Home → Blood Sugar → DIA AI → FoodChart → Health Analysis → Doctors, all reachable from the same bottom bar, with Home acting as a summary that surfaces the day's calories, steps, and water intake alongside the day's sugar reading. Nothing sits more than one tap from the bar — the app avoids nested settings-style menus because the target user is often checking in quickly, not browsing.

User flow

Log a reading → understand it → act on it

The core flow: Home → "Add Reading" → Blood Sugar screen updates → tap into DIA AI with the new reading in context → AI offers a quick-reply action (food chart / exercise / find a doctor) → user lands in the relevant screen. That last handoff was the detail I iterated on most — early versions dead-ended the AI chat, so I added the quick-reply chips specifically so a conversation always resolves into a concrete next screen instead of more typing.

Wireframes

From layout logic to final UI

Low-fidelity structure came first: bottom-nav placement, card hierarchy on Home, and where the floating "+" action should sit relative to the nav bar. The onboarding and authentication flow (below) was wireframed as a standard three-slide value proposition followed by a single login/signup decision point, kept short so it doesn't compete with the app's actual utility.

Design process & UI decisions

Blue as a clinical signal, not a brand choice

The palette runs on a single blue with green/red used only for status — "Normal" in green next to blood sugar, BMI, and heart rate; the glucose line chart in blue with clearly separated Highest/Lowest markers. Colour is doing semantic work, not decoration, which matters in a health app where a misread colour is a real cost.

The DIA AI assistant screen uses a soft illustrated robot rather than a plain chat bubble UI — a deliberate choice to make the assistant feel approachable for a first-time user who might otherwise find a health chatbot clinical or cold.

Challenges

Fitting a lot of health data without it feeling clinical

The hardest screen was Health Analysis: average glucose, HbA1c prediction, glucose trend, and calorie burn all competing for the same space. Early drafts read like a lab report. I resolved it by pulling the two headline numbers (Avg Glucose, HbA1c Prediction) into their own cards above the charts, so the page has a clear "here's the summary" moment before it asks the user to read a graph.

Outcome

Where this stands today

As a portfolio case study, the deliverable is a complete, prototyped flow across 12 screens — onboarding through booking — with a consistent design system (colour, type, iconography, and the five-tab IA) applied throughout.

Reflection

Designing the AI assistant taught me that a "smart" feature is only as good as its exits — the value wasn't in making DIA sound clever, it was in making sure every answer pointed somewhere useful. That's a lesson I carried into the coffee-ordering case study next, where the same principle applies to a much lower-stakes flow.

Next improvements

  • Add a way to export a reading history as a clinic-ready PDF before an appointment.
  • Run a moderated usability test with 5–8 real users managing diabetes and fold findings back into the flow.
