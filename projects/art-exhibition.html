Art Exhibition 2025: prototyping a gallery you move through, not scroll past.

A concept site for a digital art exhibition, designed and prototyped as a motion walkthrough instead of static screens — because the thing being tested was pacing, not layout.

RoleUI/UX & Motion Designer (solo)
PlatformWeb — immersive/scroll-based
ToolsFigma, video prototyping
Timelineadd duration

Overview

A gallery site, treated as a paced experience

Art Exhibition 2025 is a concept for a digital gallery's exhibition site — the kind of page an art institution might launch alongside a physical or hybrid show. Rather than a static homepage with a grid of thumbnails, I prototyped it as a scroll-driven sequence: each scroll interval reveals a new work, the way walking through a gallery reveals one piece at a time.

Problem

Grid layouts flatten what a gallery is for

A thumbnail grid is efficient, but it's also the opposite of how a physical exhibition is designed to be experienced — one work at a time, with intentional pacing between them. The design problem was translating that deliberate pacing into a web interaction, without it becoming a gimmick that gets in the way of actually seeing the art.

Users
  • Visitors previewing a show before deciding whether to attend in person.
  • Remote viewers who won't attend physically and want the closest available substitute for walking through the space.
Research

Looking at gallery walkthroughs, not just gallery websites

Instead of benchmarking other exhibition websites first, I looked at how physical gallery walkthroughs are paced — the spacing between works, the way a piece is framed by empty wall before and after it — and used that as the reference for scroll timing and whitespace, rather than borrowing conventions from e-commerce-style image grids.

Competitive analysis

Pattern seen

Most exhibition sites function as a catalogue — searchable, filterable, but rarely paced or sequenced.

Design response

This concept sacrifices some of that searchability deliberately, in exchange for a first-visit experience closer to actually walking the show.

Persona
A

Anika, 22 — art student deciding whether to visit

Goal: get a real feel for the show's tone before spending time travelling to see it.
Frustration: most exhibition previews are three sentences and a poster image.
What she needs: something that feels closer to actually being there.

Information architecture

A single-path structure rather than a branching one: Entrance → sequence of works → closing/about the exhibition → visit details. The concept intentionally has no secondary navigation during the walkthrough itself — wayfinding controls are reserved for before and after the sequence.

User flow & wireframes

The flow was blocked out as a storyboard before any visual design — a sequence of "beats" mapped like a shot list, each with an intended pause length, before I moved into full visual treatment and captured the result as the video walkthrough above.

Design process & UI decisions

Restraint as the main design tool

Because the artwork needs to be the visual focus, UI chrome is kept minimal throughout — no persistent header, no visible scroll indicator competing with the piece on screen. Any interface element that appears does so briefly, at a transition point, rather than staying on screen throughout.

Challenges

Motion-led concepts are hard to pin down to "correct" or "incorrect" the way a form or a checkout flow is — the main challenge was resisting the urge to add navigation "just in case," when the actual goal was proving the paced experience works before hedging it with escape hatches.

Reflection

This was the project furthest from my usual process — no persona-first, requirements-first structure, more like directing a short film. It's also the one that most changed how I think about pacing in "normal" product UI: even a settings screen has a rhythm, I just hadn't been naming it before.

Next improvements
  • Build an interactive (not just video) version so the pacing can actually be user-tested.
  • Add an accessible, non-motion fallback path for visitors with vestibular sensitivity or reduced-motion preferences.
  • Design the "visit details" close screen, currently the least developed part of the concept.
