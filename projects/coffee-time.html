02 — E-commerce · Mobile app

It's Coffee Time: designing the six taps between "I want coffee" and "it's ordered."

A coffee-ordering app case study covering the full purchase path — browse, customize, review, pay — built around how short people's patience actually is when they're ordering something this routine.

RoleUI/UX Designer (solo)
PlatformMobile — iOS & Android
ToolsFigma
Timelineadd duration
Overview

A straightforward flow, made deliberately short

It's Coffee Time is an ordering app: find a drink, set its size and quantity, review the order, pay, done. There's no loyalty program, no social feed, no distraction from the transaction — the whole case study is an argument for how few screens a good ordering flow actually needs.

Problem

Ordering apps that make a two-minute decision take five

A lot of food-ordering UI over-designs the "browse" step — endless categories, filters, and promotional banners before you can pick a drink. For something as habitual as a daily coffee order, that friction is the actual problem to solve, not a feature gap.

Users
  • Regulars who order the same one or two drinks and want the fastest possible repeat path.
  • First-time browsers who need the menu, ratings, and description visible before they'll commit to an order.
Research

Benchmarking against food-delivery patterns

I looked at how established food-delivery apps structure product detail pages — star rating and price up top, size selector as a single row of pill buttons, quantity stepper below — and adapted that same grammar for a single-vendor coffee app, where the "restaurant selection" step doesn't exist and the product page can carry more weight.

Competitive analysis

Pattern seen

Many ordering apps bury size and quantity controls behind a second modal or a separate "customize" screen.

Design response

Size (S/M/L) and quantity both live directly on the product detail screen, visible without a tap — the only modal-like step is the final review before payment.

Persona
M

Manisha, 24 — orders the same cappuccino most mornings

Goal: reorder her usual in under 30 seconds.
Frustration: apps that reset her customizations every time she reopens a product page.
What she needs: size and quantity to default sensibly, and a checkout that doesn't ask her to re-enter her address every session.

Information architecture

Home (search + popular shops) → Product detail → Review order → Payment method → Confirmation. Four steps after browsing, with a persistent bottom bar (Home, Favourites, Cart, Profile) so a half-finished order is never more than one tap from being resumed.

User flow

Search → customize → review → pay

From Home, a search bar and drink-type chips ("Cappuccino," "Latte," "Americano") narrow the list fast. Tapping a card opens the product detail screen with size and quantity already interactive — no separate "add to cart" confirmation screen — before moving to Review Order, which restates size, quantity, and price one more time as a final check before Payment.

Wireframes

Onboarding kept to three screens

Get Started → Login → Sign Up, all set against the same coffee-bean texture so the brand is established before any UI chrome appears. I wireframed the size and quantity controls separately (shown as isolated component studies in the working file) before dropping them into the product page, to test tap-target spacing on a single row of three pill buttons.

Design process & UI decisions

Warm, photographic, low on chrome

The UI leans on full-bleed coffee photography and a warm brown/cream palette rather than illustration — appropriate for a food product where the photo is doing the persuasion, not the interface. UI elements (buttons, cards, inputs) stay a flat, translucent white so they read as overlays on the photography instead of competing panels.

Challenges

Keeping legibility on photographic backgrounds was the recurring issue — white text and translucent cards can disappear against a busy coffee-bean photo. I addressed it with a consistent dark overlay behind text blocks and by reserving the full-bleed photo treatment for onboarding only, switching to solid white cards everywhere the user needs to actually read numbers (price, total, card details).

Outcome
8Screens, onboarding to confirmation
4Steps from product to payment

Reflection

This project was a useful contrast to Medi-DiaBioTrack — low-stakes, high-frequency use cases reward removing screens, while high-stakes use cases reward adding the right screen at the right moment. Knowing which situation I'm in is now one of the first questions I ask on a new brief.

Next improvements
  • Add a "reorder usual" shortcut on Home for repeat customers.
  • Design an empty-cart and order-tracking state — not yet covered in this version.
  • Test the size/quantity controls with real thumbs on a small-screen device to confirm tap targets hold up.
